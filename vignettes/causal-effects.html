Causal effects with cross-fitted double machine learning

plrcf() estimates the average effect of a treatment on an outcome when the treated and the untreated differ in measured ways (confounders). It fits the partially linear model

\[ Y = \theta D + g(X) + \varepsilon, \qquad D = m(X) + v, \]

by the double machine learning recipe of Chernozhukov et al. (2018): the outcome and the treatment are each predicted from the confounders \(X\) on folds the row was not part of (cross-fitting), and \(\theta\) is the regression of the outcome residuals on the treatment residuals (DML2), with a sandwich standard error. When the effect is the same for everyone, \(\theta\) is the average treatment effect (ATE); otherwise it is a variance-weighted average of the individual effects. exprspplr() bootstraps the same estimator for a continuous exposure (see the air-pollution vignette). The nuisances \(g\) and \(m\) are ordinary least squares fits, so \(\theta\) is consistent when they are linear in the columns given; add squares, interactions or splines as columns where they are not.

A known effect, recovered

Confounding biases the simple comparison; the cross-fitted estimate removes it. Here the true effect of d on y is 2, and x1 drives both:

set.seed(42)
n <- 2000
sim <- data.frame(x1 = rnorm(n), x2 = rnorm(n))
sim$d <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(1.5 * sim$x1))
sim$y <- 2 * sim$d + 3 * sim$x1 + sim$x2 + rnorm(n)
mean(sim$y[sim$d == 1]) - mean(sim$y[sim$d == 0])   # biased by x1
#> [1] 5.373073
fit <- plrcf(sim, "y", "d", c("x1", "x2"))
fit$ate
#> [1] 2.039439
fit$ate + c(-1.96, 1.96) * fit$se
#> [1] 1.938179 2.140699

The NSW job-training experiment, and what observational data can recover

LaLonde (1986) took the National Supported Work Demonstration, a randomised job-training programme of the 1970s, and asked whether the econometric methods of the day could recover its experimental effect on 1978 earnings when the randomised controls are replaced by a survey sample (the PSID). Dehejia and Wahba (1999) kept the men with 1974 earnings: 185 treated, 260 randomised controls, and 2,490 PSID-1 comparison men. The three files are on data.rmorie.com (rmbl login once), checked against the SHA-256 the manifest lists:

tr <- dload("nsw_dw/treated")
ct <- dload("nsw_dw/control")
ps <- dload("nsw_dw/psid_controls")

Or, without signing in, from Rajeev Dehejia’s own pages (whitespace-separated text):

cols <- c("treat", "age", "educ", "black", "hisp", "married", "nodegree", "re74", "re75", "re78")

The experimental benchmark, and the same question asked of the observational sample:

x <- c("age", "educ", "black", "hisp", "married", "nodegree", "re74", "re75")
exp <- rbind(tr, ct)
obs <- rbind(tr, ps)
mean(tr$re78) - mean(ct$re78)                 # the experiment
mean(tr$re78) - mean(ps$re78)                 # treated against the PSID, unadjusted
plrcf(exp, "re78", "treat", x)
plrcf(obs, "re78", "treat", x)
plrcf(obs, "re78", "treat", x, kfol = 10, seed = 2)

Adjusting for the eight pre-programme covariates turns a fifteen-thousand-dollar penalty into a small positive effect whose interval covers the experimental one, though it sits well below it and is far less precise. Two things limit it. The nuisances are linear, so the large earnings gap is adjusted for only as a straight line; and the overlap is thin: most PSID men earned more before the programme than almost any treated man, so the comparison rests on the few who look like them. Dehejia and Wahba show that trimming the comparison group to the region of common support (on an estimated propensity score) brings the observational estimate close to the experiment; with plrcf(), restrict obs to that region before fitting. The cross-fit split moves the estimate by about a sixth of a standard error, which is why the seed and the number of folds are arguments.

Overlap and balance

Two checks belong with every weighting estimate: whether the two groups overlap (each person has a real chance of either treatment) and whether weighting balances the covariates. On simulated data, where the true effect is 3:

set.seed(7)
n <- 1500
d <- data.frame(age = runif(n, 18, 70), income = rnorm(n))
d$t <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-2 + 0.04 * d$age + 0.5 * d$income))
d$y <- 10 + 3 * d$t + 0.2 * d$age + d$income + rnorm(n)
fit <- aipwcf(d, "y", "t", c("age", "income"), lrn = "ols")
fit
#> Cross-fitted AIPW, ATE: 1500 rows, 684 treated
#>  estimate      se lower upper p_value
#>     3.071 0.05606 2.961 3.181       0
#> Propensities from 0.086 to 0.903; 0 clipped
plot(fit)

plot(fit, type = "estimate")

pr <- attr(fit, "propensity")
e <- pmin(pmax(pr$propensity, 0.01), 0.99)
d$ipw <- ifelse(d$t == 1, 1 / e, 1 / (1 - e))
plot(baltab(d, "t", c("age", "income")),
     after = baltab(d, "t", c("age", "income"), w = "ipw"))

The groups overlap where both curves have mass; after weighting, both covariates fall below the 0.1 line.

Indigenous examples

The person-level questions this vignette is about (does a strong sense of belonging go with better mental health once circumstances are held equal?) need the survey microdata, which Statistics Canada distributes as a public use file; aipwcf() with design = svydes(...) takes its bootstrap weights directly. The published tables bundled with the package support the group-level questions; for those see vignette("first-nations-metis-inuit-health"). A comparison of the published estimates themselves:
