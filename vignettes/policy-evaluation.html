Evaluating a policy from administrative panels

A reform reaches some places before others: a new criminal procedure rolled out district by district, a policing program adopted city by city, a ward boundary redrawn. Agencies publish the outcomes as counts or rates by place and period. This vignette covers the designs that turn such a table into an estimate of the reform’s effect, with honest uncertainty. Each estimator gives the same answer as the reference implementation (fixest, did, bacondecomp) to rounding error, and none of them needs a package outside base R.

The examples use polex(): twenty districts in four regions over 36 months, with a reform that reaches the regions in three stages (months 13, 19 and 25) and never reaches the fourth. The numbers are made up, with a known effect: the reform lowers the crime rate by 3.

Difference in differences

plndid() fits the two-way fixed-effects regression, with standard errors clustered by unit (or any coarser grouping), and reports the effect relative to the comparison units’ mean before the reform:

plndid(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "reformed")
#> Difference in differences: 720 observations, 20 clusters
#>     term estimate     se  lower  upper      t  p_value
#>  treated   -3.232 0.1164 -3.475 -2.988 -27.76 7.66e-17
#> Effect relative to the comparison units' pre-period mean (26.52): -12.2%

Before any model

cohtrnd() shows each adoption cohort’s outcome around its adoption, scaled to 1 just before it:

plot(cohtrnd(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "refmonth", win = c(-10, 10)))

Leads and lags

evstudy() estimates the effect period by period around adoption. The leads (periods before adoption) should be near zero; the joint test of that is printed below the table:

es <- evstudy(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "refmonth", leads = 4, lags = 6)

Staggered adoption

When districts adopt at different times, the two-way estimate uses districts already treated as controls for later ones. bacondcmp() shows how much weight those comparisons get:

b <- bacondcmp(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "reformed")
tapply(b$weight, b$type, sum)
#> earlier vs later later vs earlier treated vs never 
#>        0.2093023        0.2093023        0.5813953
plot(b)

stgatt() avoids them: each cohort is compared only with districts not yet treated (Callaway and Sant’Anna), or the regression has a coefficient for every cohort and period (Wooldridge). The bootstrap resamples whole districts:

s <- stgatt(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "refmonth", reps = 99, seed = 1)
s$overall
#>    estimate        se     lower     upper
#> 1 -2.825844 0.3400471 -3.492324 -2.159364
plot(s)

For a treatment that can switch off as well as on, swtdid() compares the units that switch with those that do not, in the period they switch, with a placebo one period earlier:

swtdid(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "reformed", reps = 99, seed = 1)
#>      term   estimate        se     lower      upper switchers
#> 1  effect -3.1355000 0.4555572 -4.028376 -2.2426243        15
#> 2 placebo -0.5169222 0.4429911 -1.385169  0.3513245        15

Inference from the assignment

With four regions there are only 24 ways to assign the three stages and the never-treated region. perminf() tries every one, and gives the share at least as extreme as the real assignment. With two outcomes it also gives Westfall and Young’s stepdown p-values, which hold the chance of any false positive at the nominal level:

r <- perminf(p, "district", "month", c("crmrate", "arrrate"), "refmonth",
                           cl = "region")
r
#>   outcome   estimate  statistic    p_value     pstpdn assignments exact
#> 1 crmrate -3.2316395 -19.516735 0.04166667 0.04166667          24  TRUE
#> 2 arrrate -0.2136969  -3.638159 0.12500000 0.12500000          24  TRUE
plot(r, type = "histogram")

loounit() checks that no single district drives the estimate:

plot(loounit(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "reformed"))

Several outcomes at once, in the style of a Stata event-study figure:

plotevgrid(list(
  "Crime rate" = es,
  "Arrest rate" = evstudy(p, "district", "month", "arrrate", "refmonth", leads = 4,
                              lags = 6)), style = "stata")

Counts

For counts, plnpois() gives the effect as a rate ratio, with the population as exposure:

plnpois(p, "district", "month", "crimes", "reformed", offset = "logpop")
#>      term  estimate         se rateratio     lower     upper      p_value
#> 1 treated -0.092146 0.02220761  0.911972 0.8731289 0.9525431 3.334947e-05

One treated place

synctl() builds a weighted average of untreated places that tracks the treated one before the reform, and runs the same method on each untreated place as a placebo:

p13 <- p[is.na(p$refmonth) | p$district == "D13", ]
sc <- synctl(p13, "district", "month", "crmrate", "D13", adopt = 13)
sc
#> Synthetic control for D13, adoption at 13
#> Donor weights: D12 0.408, D04 0.376, D20 0.185, D16 0.031 
#> Pre-period RMSPE 0.7638; mean gap after adoption -3.107; post/pre RMSPE ratio 4.33
#> Placebo-in-space p = 0.167 (5 placebos)
plot(sc)

Measures for justice data

Three measures prepare the outcomes these designs take:

  • sevidx() combines offence rates, weighted by each offence’s severity (for example its mean statutory sentence);
  • piperate() gives the rate at which one stage of the justice process follows another, with the input’s window set before the outcome’s, because cases take time;
  • cmlrisk() gives the chance of a first arrest (or conviction, or placement) by each age, from first events and the population.

For place-based designs, bufasgn() marks places near a site as treated, places near a comparison point and far from every site as the comparison, and excludes the rest.

d <- data.frame(city = rep(c("A", "B"), each = 3), year = 2024,
                offence = rep(c("homicide", "robbery", "burglary"), 2),
                n = c(5, 120, 300, 2, 90, 410), pop = rep(c(250000, 180000), each = 3))
sevidx(d, "city", "year", "offence", "n",
               w = c(homicide = 25, robbery = 8, burglary = 4), pop = "pop")
#>   unit time unweighted weighted
#> 1    A 2024   170.0000  914.000
#> 2    B 2024   278.8889 1338.889
cmlrisk(16:20, c(900, 1400, 1700, 1600, 1400), rep(1e5, 5))

Doing better than the original designs

Published applications of these designs often share a few weaknesses, and each has a remedy here.

Few clusters. A reform assigned to a handful of districts gives cluster-robust tests that reject too often. wcb() gives an honest p-value and interval; with four clusters it says plainly that nothing can be rejected at 5%:

wcb(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "reformed", cl = "region")
#>   estimate         t p_value lower upper clusters draws enumerated
#> 1 -3.23164 -19.51674   0.125  -Inf   Inf        4    16       TRUE

Doubts about parallel trends. tse() asks how large a departure from parallel trends, relative to the largest seen before adoption, would make the effect vanish:

tse(es, seed = 1)
#> Sensitivity of the average post-period effect to departures from parallel trends (relative magnitudes)
#>  mbar lowerbnd upbound   lower   upper
#>   0.0   -3.183 -3.1828  -3.938 -2.4111
#>   0.5   -4.641 -1.7247  -6.987  0.1902
#>   1.0   -6.099 -0.2665 -10.199  3.3527
#>   1.5   -7.557  1.1916 -13.461  6.6212
#>   2.0   -9.015  2.6497 -16.729  9.8277
#> Breakdown: the interval first includes 0 at Mbar = 0.4677591

Covariates that differ between groups. stgatt() with covariates is doubly robust (use covariates fixed before treatment: one the reform moves, such as arrests, is a bad control). plndml() lets a machine learner fit the confounding with fixed effects, and mtchdid() matches units on their pre-reform paths before differencing:

set.seed(4)
p$x1 <- rnorm(20)[match(p$district, unique(p$district))]
stgatt(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "refmonth", covs = "x1", reps = 49,
              seed = 1)$overall
#>    estimate       se   lower    upper
#> 1 -2.459345 0.691214 -3.8141 -1.10459
m <- mtchdid(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "refmonth", ratio = 2)
m$estimate
#> Difference in differences: 720 observations, 20 clusters
#>     term estimate     se  lower  upper      t   p_value
#>  treated   -3.081 0.1208 -3.334 -2.829 -25.51 3.672e-16
#> Effect relative to the comparison units' pre-period mean (26.3): -11.7%

A single treated place, or a few. syndid() weights both comparison units and pre-periods:

sd <- syndid(p13, "district", "month", "crmrate", "refmonth", reps = 50, seed = 1)
sd
#> Synthetic difference in differences: -2.886 (placebo s.e. 0.5931; -4.048 to -1.723)
#> Unit weights: D12 0.207, D20 0.203, D04 0.201, D16 0.195, D08 0.195
plot(sd)

Null results without a measure of power. plnpwr() simulates the design on the untreated data and gives the minimum detectable effect:

pw <- plnpwr(p[p$month <= 12, ], "district", "month", "crmrate", ntrt = 10,
                  adopt = 7:10, eff = c(0, 0.5, 1, 1.5), reps = 40, seed = 1)
pw
#>  effect power
#>     0.0 0.050
#>     0.5 0.475
#>     1.0 1.000
#>     1.5 1.000
#> Size (rejections with no effect): 0.050 at alpha 0.050
#> Minimum detectable effect at this power: 0.8095
plot(pw)

Indigenous examples

The methods here need a panel: units observed over time, some treated at a known date. The bundled Indigenous tables are repeated cross-sections of published estimates, so the before-and-after comparison they support is the change in a gap, with its interval:

hi <- indiex("health")
x <- hi[hi$age == "Total, 18 years and over" &
          hi$indicator == "Perceived mental health, very good or excellent", ]
gaps <- do.call(rbind, lapply(split(x, x$period), function(p) {
  cbind(period = p$period[1],
        indgap(p, "group", "estimate", "lower", "upper")[
          , c("group", "difference", "diflo", "difup")])
}))
rownames(gaps) <- NULL
gaps
#>      period         group difference     diflo      difup
#> 1 2015/2018 First Nations      -10.8 -13.23311  -8.366895
#> 2 2015/2018         Métis       -9.6 -12.03311  -7.166895
#> 3 2015/2018         Inuit      -21.5 -27.31378 -15.686223
#> 4 2019/2022 First Nations      -15.7 -18.53593 -12.864070
#> 5 2019/2022         Métis       -8.7 -11.58531  -5.814692
#> 6 2019/2022         Inuit      -13.7 -20.16568  -7.234321

Perceived mental health fell for every group between the two cycles; whether the gaps moved is read from the intervals, not the point estimates.
